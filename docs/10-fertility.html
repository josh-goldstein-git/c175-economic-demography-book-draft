In this chapter. If children are a “normal” good, something people want more of as they get richer, why has fertility fallen as incomes have risen? We build two economic models of fertility choice. The first, the cost-of-time model, treats the parents’ time as part of the price of a child. 10.1 The big questions

10.1.1 A puzzle for economics, and for biology

Economists usually assume that when people get richer, they buy more of most things. Goods like that are called normal goods. If children were a normal good, richer people and richer societies would have more children. Over the last century and a half, the opposite has happened. In the United States, the total fertility rate fell from about 7 in 1800 to around 2 today, with one big interruption, the baby boom after World War II, even as incomes rose enormously.

The same puzzle troubles biology. Natural selection rewards whatever leaves more descendants. So why doesn’t social and economic success translate into more offspring?

Added · from readings Darwin, Malthus, and the Italian village example are from Becker 1991, pp. 136–137.

Becker opens his chapter with exactly this tension. Malthus assumed that populations grow rapidly unless held back by limited food. Darwin, who wrote that reading Malthus gave him “a theory by which to work,” extended the logic: the children of fertile parents make up a larger share of the next generation, so “populations tend to become dominated by the highly fertile.” But, Becker points out, “most families have controlled their fertility and have had fewer children than their capacities permit.” Seventeenth-century Italian village women who married at twenty-five averaged only six children, when their biological capacity “probably exceeded eight.” Neither Malthus nor Darwin can explain why Western fertility fell so far in the last century as family incomes “rose dramatically.”

10.1.2 Two kinds of evidence

There are two kinds of questions here.

  • Over time. Why did fertility fall during the demographic transition, and why does it keep falling?
  • Across people and places. Within a society, who has more children and who has fewer? Between societies, why is fertility high in some and low in others?

On differences across people: in Zimbabwe in 1999, the TFR was 4.0 for women as a whole, but more than 5 for women with no education and under 2 for women with higher education. (The TFR is a “period” measure, with some caveats.) Across the states of India in 2010–13, the TFR ranged from below 1.65 in some states to above 3 in others. And we would like to know what will happen in the future.

This chapter covers two economic models. The first, the opportunity cost of childbearing, gives children a price that includes the parents’ time. The second, Becker’s quantity–quality tradeoff, lets parents choose how much to invest in each child as well as how many children to have.

10.2 Consumer choice: a quick review

We all make choices between goods all the time: rent or food, books or movies, more leisure or more income. The economist’s model of these choices has three ingredients:

  1. We choose a bundle of goods together, \((X_1, X_2)\), not one at a time.
  2. Our total spending is limited by a budget constraint: \(p_1 X_1 + p_2 X_2 \le\) the available budget.
  3. Among all the bundles we can afford, we choose the one that maximizes our utility, \(U(X_1, X_2)\).

Added · from readings The definitions in the next two paragraphs follow the Wikipedia entry “Consumer choice,” recommended in the course calendar.

We picture preferences with indifference curves. Each curve connects bundles that make the person equally happy, and curves farther from the origin are better. They are usually bowed toward the origin, because people are willing to give up less and less of one good to get more of another as they have more of it. The best affordable bundle is where the highest reachable indifference curve just touches the budget line.

When a price changes, the effect on what people buy can be split into two parts. The substitution effect is the change in demand “resulting from a price change that alters the slope of the budget constraint but leaves the consumer on the same indifference curve”: people switch toward whatever has become relatively cheaper. The income effect is the change in demand from the change in purchasing power. The Wikipedia entry ends with the case that matters most for us, the trade-off between labor and leisure. A person has a fixed endowment of time, and every hour not spent working is an hour of earnings given up. Time has a price, and that price is the wage.

10.3 The cost-of-time model

10.3.1 Setting up the model

Our first model of fertility puts these pieces together.

  1. Utility depends on goods and children: \(U(X, N)\), where \(X\) is consumer goods and \(N\) is the number of children.
  2. The budget constraint: a family can spend only what it earns.
  3. Earnings depend on the wage, the hours worked, and “other income.”
  4. Time is spent either working or taking care of children. For simplicity, we leave out leisure.

The key idea is that the model counts the opportunity cost of time. For convenience, we measure goods in dollars, so the price of \(X\) is 1.

The time constraint. If \(T\) is the total hours available, \(L\) is hours in the labor force, and each child takes \(c\) hours of care, then

\[ T = L + cN. \]

The money budget. Spending on goods \(X\) plus spending on children, at \(p\) dollars per child (food, clothes, and so on), must equal earnings plus other income \(I\):

\[ X + pN = wL + I = w(T - cN) + I. \]

Moving the time cost of children to the left side gives

\[ X + (p + wc)N = wT + I. \]

This reframes the problem. The right side, \(wT + I\), is full income: what the family would have if every available hour were spent working. The left side counts all expenses, including opportunity costs. The full price of a child is \(p + wc\): the money cost plus the earnings given up to care for the child.

10.3.2 A worked example

Take the example from lecture: a wage of \(w = \$20\) an hour, \(T = 40\) hours a week, other income \(I = \$1{,}000\) a week, money costs of \(p = \$200\) per child per week, and time costs of \(c = 5\) hours per child per week. Full income is \(20 \times 40 + 1000 = \$1{,}800\) a week, and the full price of a child is \(200 + 20 \times 5 = \$300\). So the family can afford $1,800 of goods and no children, or 6 children and no goods, or anything on the line between: \(X = 1800 - 300N\).

To find the best bundle, we need preferences. Lecture uses the Cobb–Douglas utility function \(U(X, N) = X^{0.7} N^{0.3}\). Figure 10.1 shows the budget line, a few indifference curves, and utility along the budget line.

The family’s best choice here is about 1.8 children, and $1,260 a week of goods. (Children come in whole numbers, of course. Think of \(N\) as an average across many families.) In one sentence: given the budget constraint, we pick the combination of goods and children that maximizes utility.

For those who like formulas

With Cobb–Douglas utility \(U = X^a N^{1-a}\), the best choice can be found exactly (the lab shows the derivation):

\[ N^* = (1 - a)\,\frac{I + wT}{p + cw}, \qquad X^* = a\,(I + wT). \]

The family spends a fixed share \(1 - a\) of full income on children. In the example, \(N^* = 0.3 \times 1800 / 300 = 1.8\). You don’t need to memorize this.

10.4 Effects of income and wage changes

10.4.1 More non-wage income

Suppose non-wage income \(I\) rises, perhaps because of an inheritance or, in a family where only the mother cares for children, a raise for the father. The budget line shifts out parallel to itself: its slope, \(-(p + wc)\), doesn’t change. The family can afford more of both goods and children, and if both are normal goods, it buys more of both. More non-wage income raises fertility.

10.4.2 Higher wages: two effects pulling in opposite directions

A higher wage is different, because the wage appears on both sides of the budget. It raises full income, \(wT + I\), and it raises the price of a child, \(p + wc\). So the budget line becomes steeper as well as shifting out.

  • The income effect of a higher wage could raise fertility: more income to spend, including on the fixed money costs of childbearing.
  • The substitution effect could lower fertility: each hour spent with a child now costs more in lost earnings, so the family substitutes toward goods.

The effect on the number of children is ambiguous. It depends on which effect is bigger. Figure 10.2 shows both possibilities. Following lecture, we split the move from the old choice A to the new choice C into two steps. A → B is the income effect: we hold prices (the slope) fixed and move to the new level of utility. B → C is the substitution effect: we change the price and move along the new indifference curve.

decomp_plot <- function(T, I, p, c, a, w0, w1, main) {
  P0 <- full_price(p, c, w0); P1 <- full_price(p, c, w1)
  F0 <- full_income(w0, T, I); F1 <- full_income(w1, T, I)
  A <- c(N = N_star(w0, T, I, p, c, a), X = X_star(w0, T, I, p, c, a))
  C <- c(N = N_star(w1, T, I, p, c, a), X = X_star(w1, T, I, p, c, a))
  UA <- unname(util(A["X"], A["N"], a)); UC <- unname(util(C["X"], C["N"], a))
  B <- tangent_on_U(UC, P0, a)                      # new utility, old prices
  nmax <- max(F0 / P0, F1 / P1, B["N"] * 1.6)
  N <- seq(0.02, nmax, length.out = 400)

       xlab = "Number of children (N)", ylab = "Goods (X)", main = main,

  segments(0, F1, F1 / P1, 0, col = col_new, lwd = 2.5)

In the left panel, the wage rise moves the family from 3.4 to 2.4 children. In the right panel, the same doubling of the wage moves it from 0.6 to 0.9. In both cases the income effect (A → B) is toward more children and the substitution effect (B → C) is toward fewer. What differs is their relative size. Roughly, the substitution effect wins when the time cost of children is large compared with their money cost, and when much of the family’s income does not depend on the wage.

10.4.4 Is the model great, or terrible?

The effect of a wage increase can go either way. That is bad for prediction, though good for explaining things after the fact. But the model makes much stronger predictions about other changes:

  • A higher money price of children, \(p\), lowers fertility. (Would cheaper schools raise fertility?)
  • More non-wage income, \(I\), raises fertility. (What if “male” income rises?)
  • A higher time cost per child, \(c\), lowers fertility. (What about labor-saving devices for child care, like washing machines for children’s clothes?)
  • A higher wage, \(w\), is ambiguous.

So the idea that rising wages reduce fertility is based on empirical research, not theoretical necessity.

What does the evidence say about income and fertility? A recent study of the United States (Hauer and Schmertmann 2020) estimated the TFR by household income from 2010–2018. Fertility falls as household income rises, up to a point, and then turns back up among the highest-income households, for both white and nonwhite women: a U-turn. Ask yourself: in what income range does the substitution effect seem to dominate, and in what range the income effect?

10.4.5 Who bears the cost of time?

The cost of children’s time falls disproportionately on women. That gives the model a sharper prediction. Higher wages for men work mostly through the income effect and should raise fertility. Higher wages for women bring a substitution effect as well, and should lower fertility, or at least raise it less than men’s wages do. A number of older studies have found this pattern. One study of Sweden (Schultz 1985) compared the price of butter, whose production was largely women’s work, with the price of grain, largely men’s. Another, by Butz and Ward (1979), was titled “The emergence of countercyclical U.S. fertility.” When women’s wages are an important part of family income, a recession, which lowers the opportunity cost of time, could actually raise fertility.

Added · from readings The two-thirds figure, the household survey evidence, and Becker’s caution about causation are from Becker 1991, p. 140.

Becker makes the same argument. “The cost of the mother’s time is a major part of the total cost of producing and rearing children,” he writes. In the United States it contributes “about two-thirds of the total cost.” “I believe that the growth in the earning power of women during the last hundred years in developed countries is a major cause of both the large increase in labor force participation of married women and the large decline in fertility.” Because fathers have spent relatively little time on children, their rising earnings have not much affected the cost of children. Household surveys bear this out. The number of children is “strongly negatively related to the wage rate or other measures of the value of time of wives, and is more often positively rather than negatively related to the wage rate or earnings of husbands.” Becker adds a caution about causation. Part of the relationship runs from children to wages, “because women invest less in market skills and more in household skills and men do the reverse when families have more children.”

More recent evidence complicates the story. Across 15 industrialized countries, fertility used to rise when unemployment rose, in the late 1950s and 1960s. Since the mid-1970s, it has fallen when unemployment rises (Örsal and Goldstein 2018). Fertility has become “pro-cyclical” again.

10.4.6 What the cost-of-time model helps explain

  • Kuwait versus Switzerland. Both are rich, but one has high fertility and the other low.
  • Women’s share of income. Why, as an increasing share of family income comes from women, fertility might decline.
  • Female education. Why increasing female education might decrease fertility.

Added · new The interpretation of Kuwait and Switzerland in terms of the model is my addition. Your slide lists the comparison without explanation.

In the language of the model, the difference between Kuwait and Switzerland is about where income comes from. Much of Kuwait’s wealth comes from oil, which, from a family’s point of view, behaves like non-wage income \(I\): it raises income without raising the price of anyone’s time. Switzerland’s wealth comes largely from the earnings of skilled workers, women included, so getting richer there also means a higher opportunity cost of time spent with children.

10.5 The quantity–quality model

10.5.1 Two puzzles

The cost-of-time model leaves two problems.

  1. An empirical problem. Very large fertility declines have happened with fairly small economic changes (Table 10.1). Children have no close substitutes, so we would not expect demand for them to swing so much.
  2. A theoretical problem. In the cost-of-time model, the cost per child is fixed. But we know the cost of raising a child has risen enormously.

Added · from readings Becker 1991, pp. 135, 148, and 149 (footnote 12).

Becker himself had been stuck on the first problem. In his first paper on fertility, in 1960, he wrote that economic theory had “little to say about the quantitative relationship between price and quantity. There are no good substitutes for children, but there may be many poor ones.” By 1991 he saw the way out: the “interaction between quantity and quality of children,” which he called “probably the major contribution of the economic analysis of fertility.”

10.5.2 Adding quality

Becker’s idea is that parents get utility not only from the number of children, \(n\), but also from their quality, \(q\): how much is spent on each child’s schooling, health, and so on. Utility becomes \(U(X, n, q)\). (Following lecture, we use Becker’s notation \(n\) for the number of children and \(p_c\) for the cost of one unit of quality for one child, but keep \(X\) for goods, which Becker calls \(Z\).)

Assume that every child in a family gets the same quality, and every unit of quality costs the same \(p_c\). Then spending on children is \(p_c q n\), and the budget constraint goes from

\[ X + p n = \text{income} \quad\text{to}\quad X + p_c q n = \text{income}. \]

There are now three choices instead of two. Holding total income \(I\) and spending on goods \(X\) fixed, quality and quantity must satisfy

\[ q = \frac{I - X}{p_c\, n}. \]

The budget constraint is no longer a straight line. It is a curved hyperbola, like \(y = c/x\). Doubling the number of children halves the quality you can afford for each.

Added · from readings The car comparison is from Becker 1991, p. 148.

Why should children be different from other goods in this way? Becker’s answer: “a rich person might well plan to own both expensive and inexpensive cars, but is unlikely to plan on having both expensive and inexpensive children.” Every child in a family gets roughly the same treatment. So the quality chosen for one child applies to all of them, and multiplies with their number.

10.5.3 The interaction between quantity and quality

Becker’s fuller version of the budget includes three kinds of cost (p. 149):

\[ \text{total child costs} = p_n n + p_q q + p_c q n. \]

  • \(p_n\) is a price per child, of any quality: pregnancy, other fixed costs, and also the cost of contraception.
  • \(p_q\) is a price per unit of quality, regardless of the number of children: a set of encyclopedias, faster internet, things shared by all the children in a household.
  • \(p_c\) is a price that depends on both \(q\) and \(n\): school fees, visits to the doctor, new shoes.

How much does it cost to add one more child? About \(p_n + p_c q\): the fixed cost plus the quality that child will receive. How much does it cost to add one unit of quality? About \(p_q + p_c n\), because the extra quality goes to every child. So the “shadow” or “full” price of quality depends on the number of children, and the price of quantity depends on quality.

In his own words

“Since an increase in q raises the amount spent on each child, it raises the relevant cost of each child. Similarly, an increase in n raises the cost of adding to the quality of each child because a larger number of children would be affected.” (Becker 1991, p. 145)

This creates a feedback loop. Suppose something makes children a little more costly. Families have somewhat fewer children. With fewer children, quality is cheaper, so they invest more in each child. With more invested in each child, another child is more expensive, so they have still fewer. And so on, until a new equilibrium is reached.

Added · from readings Becker 1991, pp. 146–147. Your slide 16-09 shows the shape of the budget curve. The specific utility function in the figure is my choice.

A surprising implication: this feedback does not require that quantity and quality be close substitutes. On the contrary, Becker shows that “equilibrium would not be possible if they were close substitutes!” The budget curve bows in toward the origin, so a family will choose some of both only if its indifference curves bow in even more sharply. That is, if it doesn’t consider quality and quantity easy to trade off. Otherwise it would pile everything into one or the other. In Figure 10.3 the indifference curves are more curved than the budget curve, and the family settles at \(e_0\).

In the figure, a compensated doubling of the fixed cost per child moves the family from 4.3 to 3.8 children, a drop of 11 percent, while quality per child rises 32 percent. Becker argues that “a moderate increase in the fixed cost of children … that raised the initial shadow price of quantity relative to quality by only 10 to 20 percent would reduce the demand for quantity and increase the demand for quality by significantly larger percentages.”

10.5.4 A close reading: why cheaper contraception raises the cost of a child

In his own words

“Therefore, an increase in, say, the fixed cost of n, perhaps because of reduced child allowances or reduced costs of contraception, would induce a substitution away from n and toward q as well as Z … The interaction between n and q implies that the increase in q raises πn further, while the decrease in n lowers πq further, which encourages still more substitution away from n and toward q. The decrease in n and increase in q could be sizable even if the increase in the fixed cost of n were modest …” (Becker 1991, pp. 149–150)

Reduced child allowances obviously raise the cost of a child. But how can cheaper contraception do so? Think of the cost of a child as including what the family does not spend on avoiding that child. If contraceptives are expensive, avoiding a child is expensive, and having one “saves” on contraception. If contraceptives become cheap, having a child saves less, so the net cost of a child goes up.

To see how the feedback in Becker’s passage can turn a small shock into a big change, it helps to work through a numerical example.

Added · new The worked example is my construction (Cobb-Douglas preferences; closed-form solution), approved by Josh; the mechanism and quote are Becker’s (1991, pp. 149–150).

Worked example: how a small shock becomes a big change

Two families have the same preferences, \(U = a\ln n + b\ln q + g\ln z\), with \(a = 0.3\) and \(g = 0.45\). They differ only in the budget:

  • With interaction: \(c\,n + p\,n\,q + z = I\). Quality is bought for each child.
  • Without interaction: \(c\,n + p\,q + z = I\). Quality is bought once for the family (think of a set of encyclopedias).

The shock: \(b\) rises from 0.25 to 0.27, an 8% rise in how much the family values quality.

1. Without interaction (the benchmark). With a linear budget, the first-order conditions are \(a/n = \lambda c\), \(b/q = \lambda p\), \(g/z = \lambda\). Multiply each by its quantity and add: \(a + b + g = \lambda I\). So each good gets a fixed share of income:

\[ n = \frac{a}{a+b+g}\cdot\frac{I}{c}, \qquad q = \frac{b}{a+b+g}\cdot\frac{I}{p}. \]

With \(I = 100\), \(c = 10\), \(p = 1\):

\(a+b+g\) share on \(n\) \(n\) share on \(q\) \(q\)
Before (\(b = 0.25\)) 1.00 0.300 3.00 0.250 25.0
After (\(b = 0.27\)) 1.02 0.294 2.94 0.265 26.5
Change -2.0% +5.9%

Raising \(b\) affects \(n\) only by slightly shrinking the share of the budget going to children. The prices of children and quality don’t change at all.

2. With interaction. Now the budget has the term \(p\,n\,q\), so the first-order conditions are

\[ \frac{a}{n} = \lambda\,(c + p q), \qquad \frac{b}{q} = \lambda\,p n, \qquad \frac{g}{z} = \lambda . \]

The terms in parentheses are the shadow prices: an extra child costs \(c + pq\), and an extra unit of quality costs \(pn\). Solving step by step:

  1. Multiply the quality condition by \(q\): \(b = \lambda\,p\,n\,q\).
  2. Multiply the quantity condition by \(n\): \(a = \lambda c n + \lambda p n q = \lambda c n + b\), so \(\lambda c n = a - b\).
  3. Add up all spending: \(\lambda(cn + pnq + z) = (a - b) + b + g = a + g\), so \(\lambda = (a+g)/I\).
  4. Therefore

\[ n = \frac{a-b}{a+g}\cdot\frac{I}{c}, \qquad q = \frac{b}{a-b}\cdot\frac{c}{p}. \]

Now \(b\) sits inside the formula for \(n\), as \(a - b\). With \(I = 100\), \(c = 2\), \(p = 1\) (units chosen so that \(n\) is about 3):

\(a - b\) \(n\) \(q\) check: \(cn + pnq + z\)
Before (\(b = 0.25\)) 0.05 3.33 10.0 6.67 + 33.3 + 60 = 100
After (\(b = 0.27\)) 0.03 2.00 18.0 4.00 + 36.0 + 60 = 100
Change -40% +80%

An 8% rise in \(b\) is a 40% fall in \(a - b\), and \(n\) is proportional to \(a - b\). Meanwhile \(q\) is proportional to \(b/(a-b)\), which goes from 5 to 9.

3. The “20×” and where it comes from. The fall in \(n\) is 40% with interaction and 2.0% without, a ratio of about 20. The no-interaction response is exactly what the interaction family would do if its shadow prices were frozen. Facing its starting prices (\(\pi_n = 12\), \(\pi_q = 3.33\)) as fixed market prices, it would cut \(n\) by only 2.0%. So the decline splits into a first-round response of about 2% and a feedback effect of about 38% more. The feedback shows up in the shadow prices:

\(\pi_n = c + pq\) \(\pi_q = pn\) \(\pi_n / \pi_q\)
Before 12 3.33 3.6
After 20 2.00 10.0

The relative price of a child almost triples, although no market price changed: \(c\) and \(p\) are constant. Valuing quality more raises \(q\). Higher \(q\) raises the cost of a child, so \(n\) falls. Lower \(n\) lowers the cost of quality, so \(q\) rises further, and so on.

4. Caveat: the multiplier depends on \(a - b\). The multiplier is large here because \(a - b\) is small: the family values numbers only a little more than quality, so it is near the edge of wanting all quality and no quantity. For the same 0.02 rise in \(b\), the fall in \(n\) with interaction is 40% if \(a - b\) starts at 0.05, 20% if it starts at 0.10, and 10% if it starts at 0.20. The no-interaction response stays near 2% in each case. The requirement \(a > b\) for an interior solution is a version of Becker’s point that quantity and quality cannot be close substitutes.

This example uses a shift in \(b\) rather than a change in the fixed cost \(c\), as in Figure 10.3. With Cobb-Douglas preferences, a change in \(c\) does not magnify the response of \(n\) (only of \(q\)); the CES preferences behind Figure 10.3 have more curvature, which is why a compensated rise in the fixed cost there produces a larger drop in the number of children.

10.5.5 “Induced demand”: a small family planning program, a big decline in fertility

Becker uses the interaction to reconcile two sides of a long debate. One side held that family planning programs were needed before fertility would fall. The other held that the value of children had to fall first.

In his own words

“Suppose that an effective family planning program could be expected to reduce births by 10 percent because that many births are ‘unwanted.’ However, births would actually fall by a much larger percent; the interaction between quantity and quality implies that a 10-percent fall in births raises the demand for quality of children, which raises the cost of (lowers the value of) quantity and further lowers the demand for births. Although family planning might take credit for the whole decline in births because it is the initiating force, the induced increase in the demand for higher-quality children and the induced decrease in the demand for quantity of children are responsible for more than half of the decline in births.” (Becker 1991, p. 151)

To recapitulate:

  1. The cost of contraception falls, so \(n\) goes down, because there are fewer unintended births.
  2. The total price of a unit of quality falls too, since there are fewer children to provide it to, and parents buy more \(q\).
  3. But as they do, the price per child rises. This induces a further fall in \(n\).
  4. That can lead to further increases in \(q\) and declines in \(n\), until a new equilibrium is reached.

10.5.6 Is contraception the cause, or the response?

Added · from readings This subsection summarizes Becker 1991, pp. 141–144. Your slides raise contraception’s role in the model but don’t cover this evidence.

If cheaper contraception sets off the feedback loop, is the contraceptive revolution the real cause of falling fertility? Becker says no. He once thought so, but by 1991 he believed “that these improvements in birth control methods are mainly an induced response to other decreases in the demand for children rather than an important cause of the decreased demand.”

He gives two kinds of evidence. First, simple methods have always been enough to cut births substantially. Without birth control, women who married at twenty averaged about eleven live births (the Hutterites are his example), one every 26 months or so. Delaying marriage by three years, reducing the frequency of intercourse by 10 percent, and breastfeeding three months longer would cut that to about 8.7 births. Using withdrawal half the time would cut it to about 6.6. Second, societies with access to birth control did not always use it, and societies without modern methods sometimes had small families anyway. The ruling families of Europe averaged more than 5.5 births from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, though they could have had fewer with known methods. The Greeks and Romans had small families through delayed marriage, infanticide, abortion, and other means. The Jews of Florence and Leghorn halved their birth rates between 1670 and 1840, while forced to live in ghettos. And fertility began falling in the United States and Japan in the 1950s, before the pill was widely used in the United States and while it was illegal in Japan.

Becker also notes why Malthus might have put so much weight on the age at marriage. When women are having eleven births, a 10 percent rise in the age at marriage reduces births by almost three times as much as a 10 percent fall in the frequency of intercourse. Delaying marriage was simply the most effective method available.

Added · from readings The rural–urban evidence is from Becker 1991, pp. 138–139 and 152–153, including the Adam Smith quotation (p. 139, note 2).

The model also helps explain why farm families have been larger than city families. The evidence covers centuries. In 1427, the average household in the city of Florence was about 20 percent smaller than in the surrounding countryside. In 1800, the rural reproduction rate in the United States was about one and a half times the urban rate. Part of the reason is that food and housing are cheaper on farms. But Becker believes “farm families have had more children mainly because children have been considerably more productive on farms than in cities.” In rural India and Brazil, children begin contributing to farm work by age five or six. Adam Smith wrote of colonial America: “Labour is there so well rewarded that a numerous family of children, instead of being a burthen is a source of opulence and prosperity to the parents.” As agriculture mechanized, farm children spent more time in school, and rural schooling was costly. So the cost advantage of farm children narrowed, and rural fertility in some advanced countries has fallen slightly below urban fertility.

10.5.8 Why don’t recessions bring fertility back?

We have seen fertility generally fall over time as wages rise. Why doesn’t it rise again in an economic crisis, when the opportunity cost of time falls? The quantity–quality model offers one possible answer. Faced with a loss of income, parents could spend less by investing less in each child, or by having fewer children. But social norms about child quality may be irreversible. Once a family, or a society, expects a certain level of schooling, health care, and so on for every child, the way to save is to have fewer children.

10.6 Putting the models together

  • The quantity–quality interaction is good for explaining the demographic transition: rapid, large fertility declines.
  • The cost-of-time model is good for explaining more recent trends, especially as women’s wages rise.
  • It is not either/or. A full treatment combines both models.

So how could fertility fall with economic growth? There are three possible answers:

  1. It doesn’t, because the income effect dominates.
  2. It does, because the substitution effect, the cost of time, dominates.
  3. Parents get utility from quality too. So once fertility starts falling, there is a big shift toward quality.

Added · from readings Becker 1991, p. 154.

Becker draws a broader lesson. “Economic development can have significant negative effects on fertility even when the ‘true’ income elasticity of demand for fertility is positive and sizable.” Richer families can have fewer children than poorer families, because development also raises the returns to investing in children.

Some mysteries remain. Why don’t we just have one child and invest a lot in her? Why has fertility become more pro-cyclical in recent years? And why hasn’t U.S. fertility recovered from the recession?

10.7 Key ideas

  • Fertility has fallen as incomes have risen, a puzzle if children are a normal good, and for biology too.
  • The cost-of-time model gives children a full price of \(p + wc\): money costs plus the earnings given up to care for them. Full income is \(wT + I\).
  • More non-wage income raises fertility. Higher money or time costs lower it. A higher wage has opposing income and substitution effects, so its effect is ambiguous in theory.
  • Because child care falls mostly on women, higher women’s wages tend to lower fertility more than men’s wages do.
  • In Becker’s quantity–quality model, spending on children is quality × quantity, so the price of each depends on the other. This interaction magnifies small changes in costs into large swings in the number of children and investment per child.
  • The interaction explains “induced demand”: an initial fall in births, from family planning for example, sets off a further fall.
