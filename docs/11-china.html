In this chapter. We turn from theories of fertility to real policy changes. This chapter looks at China’s one-child policy, perhaps the most ambitious attempt by any government to control births, and asks the hard question it shares with the next chapter: how can we tell what a policy caused, when so much else was changing at the same time?

Readings this chapter draws on. Hesketh, Lu, and Xing (2005), “The Effect of China’s One-Child Family Policy after 25 Years”; Zhang (2017), “The Evolution of China’s One-Child Policy and Its Effects on Family Outcomes.”

Added · new Bridge text written for the chapter split.

This is the first of two chapters on fertility policy. Both ask what a policy change did to births, and both face the same problem of finding a counterfactual. This chapter takes up China’s one-child policy; Chapter 12 turns to abortion and the pill in the United States.

11.1 Policy and fertility: did it matter, and how would we know?

In earlier chapters we studied theories of fertility decline. Now we look at two cases (this chapter and Chapter 12) where governments or courts deliberately changed the rules, and ask what happened to births. Both cases are controversial, and both are useful for the same reason: they force us to think carefully about causation.

The problem is simple to state. Fertility was falling in China before the one-child policy, and it was falling in the United States before Roe v. Wade. If births fell after a policy, did the policy cause the fall, or would it have happened anyway? To answer, we need a counterfactual: what would have happened without the policy. We can never observe it directly. The art is in finding a comparison that stands in for it.

Speed of decline is one place to start. Across the world, the time it took fertility to fall from more than 6 children per woman to fewer than 3 has shrunk dramatically. In the United Kingdom it took 95 years (1815–1910). In the United States it took 82 years. In China it took 11 years (1967–1978), and in Iran only 10 (1986–1996). Notice China’s dates: its fall from 6 to 3 children per woman was finished before the one-child policy began in 1979. And Iran achieved an even faster decline with no one-child policy at all.

11.2 The one-child policy: beginnings and end

11.2.1 Before the one-child policy: “Later, Longer, Fewer”

Added · from readings Mao’s views, Ma Yinchu, the 1971 slogan, and the “menstrual monitors” quotation are from Zhang 2017, pp. 142–143.

China’s population policy did not begin in 1979. Mao Zedong, China’s leader from 1949 to 1976, “believed in the principle: ‘More people, more power,’” as Junsen Zhang puts it. In the mid-1950s the economist Ma Yinchu, president of Peking University, recommended family planning. Mao was briefly supportive, but during the Great Leap Forward (1958) he reverted to the belief that a larger population was better, and Ma was denounced as a “rightist.” After the famine of the Great Leap Forward, fertility rebounded to more than six births per woman in the early 1960s.

By the end of the 1960s, China’s population passed 800 million and the economy was stagnating. A serious family planning campaign began in 1971 with the slogan “One child isn’t too few, two are just fine, and three are too many.” In 1973 it was formalized as the Later–Longer–Fewer campaign (wan–xi–shao):

  • Later marriage. People were encouraged to marry three years after the legal minimum ages: at least 25 (rural) or 27–28 (urban) for men, and at least 23 (rural) or 25 (urban) for women. Couples who married late got 7 to 30 days of “late marriage leave.”
  • Longer spacing. At least four years between births.
  • Fewer children. No more than two per couple.

The campaign worked. China’s fertility fell by half during the 1970s. Hesketh and colleagues put the total fertility rate (TFR) at 5.9 in 1970 and 2.9 in 1979; Zhang gives 5.8 in 1970 and 2.7 in 1978. By 1978, Zhang reports, urban fertility was already down to 1.55 children per woman, and rural fertility to 2.97.

The campaign was officially voluntary, but it had coercive elements. Zhang quotes the sociologists Martin Whyte, Wang Feng, and Yong Cai:

“These birth planning enforcers kept detailed records on each woman of child-bearing age under their responsibility, including past births, contraceptive usage and even menstrual cycles, in many reported instances becoming ‘menstrual monitors’ who tried to detect out-of-quota pregnancies at an early stage.”

Still, Zhang concludes, the 1970s campaign was “significantly less coercive than the one-child policy that followed.”

11.2.2 From Later–Longer–Fewer to just one child

When Deng Xiaoping came to power in 1978, the new leadership’s priority was the economy. A group of control theorists argued that China would achieve the highest level of well-being around 2050 if its population could be reduced to 650–700 million. Local governments began moving toward a one-child rule in late 1979. The policy was formally announced in September 1980, in “An open letter to all party members and all youth league members” published in the People’s Daily. The letter said that the State Council “advocates that each couple should have only one child,” and promised that after 30 years the policy might be changed.

Added · from readings Hesketh et al. 2005, p. 1171; Zhang 2017, p. 144.

Hesketh and colleagues summarize the government’s reasoning:

In their own words

“At the time, China was home to a quarter of the world’s people, who were occupying just 7 percent of world’s arable land. Two thirds of the population were under the age of 30 years, and the baby boomers of the 1950s and 1960s were entering their reproductive years. The government saw strict population containment as essential to economic reform and to an improvement in living standards.” (Hesketh, Lu, and Xing 2005)

Zhang adds that Deng had set a goal of quadrupling GDP per capita by the end of the century. Fewer people was one way to raise income per person. The policy was launched, Zhang writes, “in the context of both a neo-Malthusian concern over population and ambitious economic development goals.”

11.2.3 How the policy actually worked

Added · from readings Details of enforcement are from Hesketh et al. 2005, pp. 1171–1172, and Zhang 2017, pp. 144–145, including footnote 2.

The name “one-child policy” is misleading. “Despite its name,” Hesketh and colleagues write, “the one-child rule applies to a minority of the population.” In practice there was a two-tier system:

  • Urban residents and government employees were held strictly to one child. Many worked for state-owned enterprises and could lose their jobs and benefits if they had a second.
  • Rural residents, about 70 percent of the population, were generally allowed a second child after five years, often only if the first was a girl, “a clear acknowledgment of the traditional preference for boys.” Ethnic minorities and remote areas could have a third.

Enforcement was in the hands of local officials, who used rewards and penalties: economic incentives for compliance, and fines, confiscation of belongings, and dismissal from work for violations. The policy relied on near-universal contraception and abortion. By the early 2000s, 87 percent of married women used contraception, and IUDs and sterilization made up more than 90 percent of methods. Eighty percent of women in one large study said they had no choice of method and simply accepted what the family-planning worker recommended. Zhang notes that the heavy-handedness peaked in 1983, with “a nationwide campaign of mandatory sterilization, abortion, and IUD insertion.”

The policy also changed over time:

  • Flexibility and decentralization (1984–1989). Because of “practical difficulties,” especially resistance from rural families whose only child was a girl, the Party allowed more exceptions. The guiding phrase was “open a small hole to close up a big one.” Fertility rebounded somewhat in 1984–1986.
  • Restrengthening (from 1989). The policy was tightened again and tied to the careers of local officials: “No achievements, no promotion.” Family planning was listed among the three basic state policies in the 8th Five-Year Plan in March

The fines varied enormously from province to province and over time, as you can see in the data economists have collected on them. That variation will turn out to be useful for research.

11.2.4 The end of the one-child policy

The policy was relaxed in steps:

  • 1984–2011: local governments allowed two children for couples in which both parents were themselves only children.
  • 2014: two children allowed if either parent was an only child.
  • 2016: a universal two-child policy. The abolition of the one-child policy was announced on October 29, 2015.
  • 2021: a three-child policy, which lifted limits on third births.

Official motivations for the switch included a lower population growth rate, the recent decline in the size of the labor force, the challenges of an aging society, a lasting imbalance in the sex ratio, and the promise made in the 1979 open letter. Official propaganda has turned around completely. Banners that once read “Strictly limit second births, totally eradicate third births” and “One child is best, the government aids in elder care” now read “Three children are better than two” and “Three children are best, no need for state-supported elder care.”

If you were shown only the curve and asked to find the year the one-child policy began, it would be hard to pick it out. The big drop came in the 1970s. Afterward, fertility fell much more gradually, to around 1.8 by the mid-1990s, and then roughly stabilized. Zhang puts the numbers side by side: the TFR fell from 5.8 to 2.7 between 1970 and 1978, but from 2.8 to 1.8 between 1979 and 1995 under the one-child policy. Whyte, Wang, and Cai calculate that roughly three-quarters of the decline in fertility from 1970 to the present occurred before the one-child policy began.

11.3.2 Comparison countries

Of course, fertility might have risen after 1979 without the policy. So we need a comparison. Zhang compares rural and urban China with four other developing countries that also had high fertility in the 1960s: South Korea, Thailand, Mexico, and India. None had a one-child policy. All saw rapid declines after 1970.

Added · from readings Zhang 2017, pp. 146–148.

South Korea and Thailand, which had fast economic growth and voluntary family planning programs, fell more slowly than urban China in the 1960s and 1970s, but by 2010 their fertility was as low as China’s. Thailand, Zhang notes, “is especially worth noting because it had fertility and GDP per capita similar to China’s by 2010.” Even India, with a weaker family planning program and much slower growth, saw a marked decline. Zhang’s conclusion: “Given that the economic growth rate of China was higher than those of the four other countries from 1979, I believe that even without the one-child policy, China’s fertility rate would have declined substantially after 1978.”

Another comparison, shown in lecture, is Taiwan, which never had a one-child policy. Taiwan’s fertility followed a path quite similar to mainland China’s and has since fallen even lower.

A related observation: China was “ahead of the curve.” When we plot fertility against GDP per capita, China’s fertility fell to low levels at an exceptionally low income. Zhang points out that in China, India, South Korea, and Thailand fertility fell sharply before GDP per capita reached $3,000. China’s economy was nearly stagnant in the 1970s, so economic development cannot explain the fertility decline of that decade. The family planning campaign was “widely believed to be the main contributor.” Some demographers called it an “induced fertility transition.”

11.3.3 How economists try to identify the effect

Added · from readings The four approaches and their assumptions are from Zhang 2017, pp. 148–150.

Zhang describes four strategies that researchers have used, each with its own assumption:

  1. Before and after 1979. Compare births before and after the policy, controlling for a time trend. This assumes that any break from the trend after 1979 is due to the policy, not to the economic reforms that began at the same time.
  2. Variation in fines. Compare places with higher and lower penalties. The problem is that local governments may set fines according to local conditions and local demand for children. Fines are not randomly assigned.
  3. Han versus minorities. Han Chinese women were held strictly to the policy, while minority women were often allowed two children. Comparing the change in Han fertility to the change in minority fertility is a difference-in-differences design. It assumes the two groups would otherwise have followed the same trend, which economic reforms may have violated.
  4. Variation in enforcement intensity. Compare prefectures that enforced the policy more and less strictly (measured by “excess” births above the one-child rule), and cohorts with more and less exposure.

The ideal, Zhang observes wryly, “would have been useful for research purposes if China had randomly selected a cross-section of prefectures to implement the one-child policy and then forbidden interprefecture migration.” That never happened, so every estimate rests on assumptions.

11.3.4 What the evidence says

The studies agree that the policy did reduce fertility, especially at first. Ahn (1994), for example, found that the probability of a second child in urban areas fell by 50 percent in Hebei and Shaanxi and by 86 percent in Shanghai after the policy. But most studies find the effect was “rather small, especially in the longer-term.” Zhang offers a reconciliation:

In their own words

“A plausible reconciliation of these views is that the one-child policy accelerated the already-occurring drop in fertility for a few years, but in the longer term, economic development played a more fundamental role in leading to and maintaining China’s low fertility level.” (Zhang 2017)

Hesketh and colleagues reach the same view. China’s neighbors, without such policies, had some of the lowest fertility rates in the world: 1.04 in Singapore, 1.38 in Japan, 0.91 in Hong Kong. “It is reasonable to speculate,” they write, “that there would have been a further decline in China’s total fertility rate had the voluntary policy of the 1970s continued.” A Bayesian projection by Wang, Cai, and Gu, quoted by Zhang, concluded that without the policy, “By 2010, fertility would have fallen to its currently observed level of around 1.5 children per woman.”

That raises an awkward point about official claims. Chinese authorities claimed the policy prevented 250 to 300 million births. But that claim depends on assuming fertility would have stayed high without the policy. Wang and colleagues concluded, in Zhang’s summary, that “China’s government exaggerated the effect of the one-child policy.”

11.3.5 Fewer children, better educated children?

Added · from readings Zhang 2017, pp. 152–153.

If families can have only one child, they might invest more in that child. This is the quantity–quality trade-off from Gary Becker’s theory of fertility. Did the one-child policy produce better-educated children?

An ingenious test uses twins. In urban Kunming, where the policy was strictly enforced, about 95 percent of households without twins had one child, while households whose first birth was twins necessarily had two. Twins are a matter of chance, so comparing these families isolates the effect of an extra child. Mark Rosenzweig and Zhang found that the policy-induced fall in fertility had “a moderately positive effect on children’s human capital at most”: it raised schooling by at most 4 percent and the probability of attending college by less than 9 percent. Other studies, using variation in enforcement, find similarly small effects on education.

11.3.6 What lessons for other countries?

Zhang’s verdict on the policy as a whole is blunt. It “may have mildly accelerated the fertility transition,” but “it also brought substantial costs, including political costs, human rights concerns, a more rapidly aging population, and an imbalanced sex ratio resulting from a preference for sons. In retrospect, one may question the need for introducing the one-child policy in China.” For other countries:

“If the country is already experiencing a decline in fertility rates, then a one-child policy doesn’t seem useful. … Rather than focusing on a one-child policy, a developing country may want to consider an aggressive but less-coercive family planning program such as the policy implemented in China in the 1970s.” (Zhang 2017)

11.4 Fewer daughters: an unintended consequence?

11.4.1 The sex ratio at birth

In every population, slightly more boys are born than girls. Hesketh and colleagues report that the sex ratio at birth ranges from 1.03 to 1.07 boys per girl in industrialized countries. In China, the reported ratio rose from 1.06 in 1979 to 1.11 in 1988 and 1.17 in 2001, and reached 1.3 in some rural provinces. Figure 11.2 shows how the ratio varied by birth order.

The pattern fits the policy’s rules closely. In rural areas, where a second child was usually allowed (especially after a girl), the first-birth ratio was normal, 1.05, but it rose steeply for later births. In urban areas, where only one child was allowed, the ratio was already high, 1.13, for first births. Hesketh and colleagues explain:

“The picture that emerges is that some urban Chinese make the choice to perform sex selection with the first pregnancy, since they are allowed only one child. In rural areas, most couples are permitted to have a second child, especially if the first is female. So if the second (or subsequent) child is female, the pregnancy often ‘disappears,’ allowing the couple to have another child in an attempt to have a son.”

11.4.2 Stopping rules can’t change the sex ratio. Behavior can.

Could parents raise the share of boys simply by deciding when to stop having children? For example, “keep having children until you get a boy.” In class we test this with coins. The lesson: stopping rules alone can’t change the sex ratio. Each birth is still about as likely to be a boy as a girl, whatever the parents plan to do next. Stopping rules can, however, amplify gender inequality in other ways. (Think about how many brothers and sisters a typical boy or girl has under such a rule.)

So if we see the sex ratio change, it must be due to behavior that affects which births are born or counted:

  • sex-selective abortion, made possible by ultrasound;
  • differential reporting, or “hiding girls” from census takers and officials;
  • differential mortality, if girls receive less care;
  • and possibly other channels.

Added · from readings Hesketh et al. 2005, pp. 1172–1173; Zhang 2017, pp. 153–154.

The readings suggest that all three behaviors played a role. Sex-selective abortion “undoubtedly accounts for a large proportion of the decline in female births,” Hesketh and colleagues write, although it is illegal and hard to measure. Underreporting was large too. A 1995 household survey in three provinces found a normal sex ratio among children under 14, with the actual number of girls exceeding the number registered by 22 percent. Daniel Goodkind estimated that 19 percent of all children aged 0–4 in 2000 were unreported to the authorities. Infanticide of girls is “probably very rare now,” but “less aggressive treatment of sick female infants is known to occur.”

Was the policy itself to blame? Only partly, say Hesketh and colleagues. China had a high sex ratio in the 1930s and 1940s, and other Asian countries with son preference and falling fertility, but no one-child policy, also saw imbalances: Taiwan (1.19), Singapore (1.18), South Korea (1.12), and parts of northern India (1.20). Most tellingly, “the marked increase in the sex ratio between the 1980s and 1990s coincided with much easier access to cheap ultrasonography and not with any substantial change in enforcement of the one-child policy.” The policy amplified son preference, but technology made acting on it easy.

11.4.3 Other consequences

Hesketh and colleagues list several more consequences:

  • A marriage squeeze. The shortage of women has left some men unable to marry. The authors link it to the kidnapping and trafficking of women for marriage.
  • The “4:2:1” problem. With few children and longer lives, a growing number of couples will be solely responsible for one child and four parents. About 70 percent of elderly people still depended financially on their children.
  • Health risks. Women with unapproved pregnancies often avoided prenatal care and gave birth at home. One study found that maternal deaths doubled for unapproved pregnancies.
In the lab

In Lab 8 you use a sample of the 1990 Chinese census, about 200,000 households, to look for traces of the one-child policy in the sexes of children. You compare the proportion female by birth order and year of birth, and you ask whether parents’ decisions to have another child depended on the sexes of the children they already had. Keep in mind that the 1990 census is known to undercount young children, and that we see only surviving children.

11.5 Key ideas

  • To know what a policy caused, we need a counterfactual. Comparison countries, comparison groups, and natural experiments stand in for it.
  • China’s fertility fell by half during the voluntary but coercive Later–Longer–Fewer campaign of the 1970s, before the one-child policy. The policy itself seems to have had a modest, mostly short-term effect.
  • China’s sex ratio at birth rose sharply, especially for later births in rural areas and first births in cities. Stopping rules cannot change the sex ratio; sex-selective abortion, underreporting, and differential care can.
